### **L01/02: INTRODUCTION TO HISTOLOGY AND EPITHELIAL TISSUE** 

Histo means tissue in Greek. Histology is the study of the microanatomy of cells, tissues and organs as seen through a microscope, in particular the correlation between structure and function. 

Even though we have microscopes since the 17th century, histology is quite a recent study, because only in the 19th century we could stain cells and make them visible. 

### **TISSUES** 

Cells and extracellular components of the various organs of the body exhibit a very defined and often distinctive pattern in accordance with the function they must accomplish. 

A **tissue** is an organized aggregation of cells that function in a collective manner. 

When talking about cells we refer to a diameter in the scale of 5 to 10 µm, some cells can be larger and go up to 25 µm. Instead, when talking about tissues, we’re talking about much larger portions, we can get up to millimeters when we talk about epithelial tissue and even more complex and more structured components. For the different cells to work together they must be able to communicate: this happens through specialized intercellular junctions (gap junctions), subcellular structures, present in every cell. 

### **Histological preparation of a tissue** 

For obvious reasons, the histological preparations that are given to students usually derive from small animals (pigs, guinea pigs, mice, rats); organs of humans are much larger and when cut in pieces, it’s hard to understand where the tissue comes from. The way to prepare the sample is identical independently from the species it comes from. 

1. **Fixing/ processing** : first, we need to preserve the tissue from deterioration by fixing it, using a variety of cross linkers, chemical different substances (in ancient times ethanol and formalin). The fixing process kills the tissue. Then we need to process it treating it mechanically so that it does not break down on the slide. 

2. **Section/slide prep** : next, we must embed the tissue such that we can cut it and section it in a specific way (there are different procedures and instruments that can be used to embed different types of tissues). 

3. **Staining process** : the tissue section isn’t in its original color, so to visualize its characteristics staining is needed: each type of staining is very specific for defining molecules and structures, as it is made by colorimetric reactions, that use a method which is based on the pH and the composition of the tissue. 

4. Then we can study our tissue thanks to **brightfield microscopy** . 

### **Tissues as functional units** 

**Cells** interact with each other and with the extracellular material forming a **tissue** that has a specific function, then several tissues combine forming **organs** and then the interaction between them lead to the formation of an **organ system** . 

Each basic tissue is defined by a set of general morphological characteristics of its various cell types and any special extracellular substances that might be present.

The main function of the epithelial tissue is to create a barrier between the external and the internal environment and to protect the internal cavities. The external part of our body is the most subject to shocks and damage, so if the epithelium was vascular, the bleeding would have been excessive. The internal part of our body, on the other hand, must be protected from the activity of the organism itself. For example, in the gastrointestinal tracts, where food is ingested and then digested, the presence of acid pH and enzymes could damage the organs. 

Being that much exposed to risks, epithelial cells must self-renew very quickly. 

DNA must therefore replicate regularly, increasing the risk of mutations. Risk factors are many, including UV rays and smoking. 



The lack of blood vessels can be seen in the pink area of the figure, which corresponds to the epithelial tissue, while the blue area, which corresponds to the underlaying connective tissue, contains them. The red dots are the nuclei of each cell, and we can see they are close to each other in the epithelium, whereas they are not in the connective tissue. Another aspect we can notice is that epithelial tissue has no extracellular matrix and therefore the cells are in close contact with each 

other. This is because the epithelium acts as a selective barrier that protects the internal part of the body from pathogens. 

### **Functions** 

1. **Physical protection** and **isolation** : by forming sheet-like cellular investments that separate underlying or adjacent connective tissue from the environment, internal cavities or fluid connective tissue (as blood and lymph). It is a selective barrier as said before. 

2. **Semi-permeability** : the ability to exchange of substances between tissue and environment. 3. **Sensitivity** : there are specific epithelial cells that must sense or respond to the environment (such as the epithelial cells in the inner ear, which perform the role of mechanical receptors or osmoreceptors). These cells trigger a response to the underlying tissue, so the barrier must be selective; if there was complete isolation our body would never be aware of what happens outside. 

4. **Secretion** from specialized cells called glands. 

### **Features** 

1. Adjacent cells are **closely apposed** , so there is no extracellular matrix. 

2. Cells are **anchored to a basement membrane** , a non-cellular protein-polysaccharide rich layer appreciable at the microscope light level by histochemical methods. The epithelia is just above the connective tissue: physical communication is needed to synergistically respond to the environment. The **apical surface** of the epithelial cells is free (in contact with the lumen or with the external environment), so, if the two tissues were not connected, the function of the epithelia would be irrelevant. 

3. **Specializations on the apical part** (villi, cilia). 

4. **Baso-lateral intercellular junctions** interconnect two cells that are next to each other; this could either be between epithelial cells, so we can have many layers of epithelial cells, or between the last basal layer of epithelial cells with the underneath connective tissue 

5. It is **avascular** .

### **MATRIX FIBERS** 

**FIBROBLASTS** are the cells responsible for the **synthesis of all the fibers** (elastic, collagen, reticular fibers) in CT and the complex carbohydrates of the ECM. 

- Collagen Fibers: Most abundant. Large fibers made of  collagen. Allow tissue support, connection and flexibility 

- Reticular Fibers: Small, branched fibers filling spaces  and providing thin stroma of organs 

- Elastic Fibers: Intermediate fibers made of elastin.  Branching fibers that allow tissues to respond to stretch  and distension 

These cells produce all the **components for the ECM** in which they are dispersed into. They also secrete, maintain and recycle the matrix in most tissues. 

In general, the **ECM** is composed by: 

- **FIBRILLARY COMPONENTS** : collagen, reticular and elastic fibers; 

- **GROUND SUBSTANCE** : water, electrolytes, glycoproteins, glycosaminoglycans, proteoglycans, enzymes (necessary for production of fibers). 

### **COLLAGEN FIBERS** 

- Collagen fibers are the most important and abundant fibers (around 90% of all the total proteins in our body). 

- It’s a large, tightly packed fiber, arranged in a liner structure. 

- Its diameter is about 1-2 μm. 

- It’s a long, flexible and non-branched fiber, made of collagen proteins (mainly type I). 

- It’s responsible for tensile strength, support, connection, and flexibility. 



- They don’t have much extension, but they possess a great ability to resist mechanical stress. 

Different collagen fibers come from different ways in which collagen is formed. Different types of tissue have **different types of collagens** (type I – type XXV): 

- Collagen I:  (the most abundant collagen) skin, bone, tendon, cornea; 

- Collagen II: cartilage; 

- Collagen III: reticular fibers, loose CT, blood vessel walls, dermis; 

- Collagen IV: basal lamina of epithelium, kidney glomeruli 

### **Collagen crimps** 

Collagen crimps are irregular “waves” of collagen fibrils. In tendons, their linear segment is long 50 micrometers. There is a close relationship between this crimp architecture and its biochemical properties, that allows it to provide different levels of resistance to mechanical stress.

### **Multi adhesive glycoproteins** 

Amongst multi adhesive glycoproteins there are: 

- **Fibronectin** : a linear protein able to link cells to different components of the ECM; it is recognized by cells as a ligand for cell surface adhesive receptors (integrin family). The interaction between fibronectin and integrins is responsible for cell adhesion and is involved in cell migration (examples of migration can be: movement of immune cells to the site of inflammation, wound healing, embryogenesis and generally during growth and development) 



In the image you can see the intracellular part of a cell, protein transmembrane binding site for fibronectin; thanks to this binding there is reorganization of actin microfilaments and cytoskeleton in the cells, allowing cell to migrate. 



- **Laminin** has a different shape; it is able to bind collagen and components of the ECM and integrins, the cell surface adhesive receptors; its peculiarity is to link and bind basement membrane, indeed cells recognize receptors that interact with specific ECM components, primarily with laminin. 

### **CLINICAL INSIGHT** 



**Mucopolysaccharidosis** is a huge branch of very severe diseases caused by a rare genetically inherited disorder; the symptoms are in the image and result from the defect of the lysosomal enzymes, that are not able to degrade the very complex GAGs and proteoglycans that are present in the ground matrix; the accumulation of these affects every district of human body. Recurrent respiratory infections, heart murmur, enlarged belly, hernias, expectancy of life is very poor. The mucopolysaccharidosis has always been investigated and some gene therapy has been discovered but it is still not treated. 

When discussing connective tissue, it is important to consider its **mechanical properties** , which are determined by its structural components. The three key properties are: 

- **Tensile Strength: c** onnective tissue must withstand forces from multiple directions, resisting pulling, stretching, and tearing. This strength is provided by **collagen fibers** , which give the tissue its durability and robustness. 

- **Elasticity** : connective tissue must be able to return to its original shape after mechanical stress. This property is ensured by **elastic fibers** , which allow tissues to stretch and recoil. 

- **Volume:** the **ground substance** , composed of **carbohydrates and glycoproteins** , fills the spaces between fibers and cells. These macromolecules form a hydrated gel, giving connective tissue its volume and facilitating nutrient exchange.

### **TYPES OF MEMBRANES** 

### **_BASEMENT MEMBRANE_** 

It is a sheet-like arrangement of ECM proteins which act as an interface between the support tissue and epithelial and parenchymal cells. It is associated with blood vessels and muscle cells. It provides physical binding of epithelium and connective tissue and physical support. The main function of the basement membrane is covering broad areas, and compartmentalizing different districts of our body, it consists of an epithelial sheet plus underlying connective tissue. 

It controls epithelial growth and differentiation: this only breaches in case epithelia undergo malignant transformation (cancer). It allows the flow of nutrients, metabolites and molecules to epithelium, devoid of blood vessels. It regulates permeability (particularly relevant barrier in the kidney, which is selective). Main components are: heparan sulfate, type IV collagen, fibronectin and laminins   There are four different types: 

1. **Mucus membranes** , lining the digestive, respiratory, urinary and reproductive tracts, coated with secretions that are produced by the mucous glands (remember that along the entire tracts there are goblet cells in glands secreting mucus, crucial to lubricate the membranes, made of epithelial and connective tissue). 

2. **Serous membranes** , which line body cavities closed to the exterior of the body, like pericardial (around the heart), peritoneal (in the abdomen) and pleural, covering our body cavities; lying on the areolar connective tissue the epithelium is simple squamous; example of this serous membrane is the parietal pleura, a layer that covers the lungs and is very slippery and viscous. 

3. **Cutaneous membranes** , or the skin, covering the body surface. 

4. **Synovial membranes** line the joint cavities in order to protect them, allow for communication and produce a fluid that acts as a lubricant and as a shock absorber cushion; if there is an inflammation or injury the production can become excessive and the area swollen and painful; there is loose connective (areolar) tissue and simple squamous epithelium.

### **L11/12: CARTILAGE** 

### **SUPPORTIVE CONNECTIVE TISSUE CARTILAGE** 

What can we see in this image? 

- A fair amount of space between the cells 

- Irregularly shaped cells 

- Large, round nuclei 

- Particular distribution: clusters are formed 

- The density of cells seems to be higher than in the connective tissue proper 

- Density also increases towards the middle with bigger, denser nuclei here 



- No blood vessels 

Cartilage is a **supportive** connective tissue and provides a combination of strength and flexibility, different from bone tissue and it’s generally resistant, flexible, and quite soft. It is composed of chondroblasts who then mature in chondrocytes, and a highly specialized ECM. So, its main components are: 

- Cells (chondrocytes, chondroblasts) 

- Fibers (fibrous fibers composed of collagen, elastic fibers composed of elastin) Giving strength and flexibility 

- Extracellular matrix (ECM) 

Traditionally, - _blast_ indicates a cell that is still developing, whereas - _cyte_ indicates a fully differentiated state, however, this is a debatable definition because we don’t always have information on the replication of a cell. In some textbooks, one might find chondrocytes to indicate any type of cartilage cell. 

Chondrocytes produce extracellular matrix, collagen and elastin which provide strength and flexibility. Cartilage is the only connective tissue that is **avascular** (it doesn’t contain blood vessels) and there is also **no innervation** . 

### **Three types of cartilage** 

The classical nomenclature implies the presence of 3 types of cartilage (there may also be some intermediate types): 

- 1) Hyaline: 

   - a) Most abundant type (90%) 

   - b) Reduces friction and absorbs shock 

   - c) Larynx and trachea joints: they must be flexible enough to accommodate changes in conformations, and contemporarily they must have a certain resistance to stress. **Clinical Insight:** Tracheotomy (inserting a tube in the patient’s trachea to allow him to breathe) practice had an increase in last times. This provided new needs for medical devices and mechanisms to repair cartilage, including bioprinting strategies. 

65 

- 2) Elastic: 

   - a) Outer ear and epiglottis 

   - b) Provides shape and support 

- 3) Fibrous 

   - a) Intervertebral disks and pubic symphysis 

   - b) Provides rigidity and absorbs shock 

### **CARTILAGE COMPOSITION** 

In cartilage, we can observe a particular distribution of cells and extracellular matrix: the cartilage is composed of **5% in weight of cells** (chondroblasts, chondrocytes) and **95% extracellular matrix** . The extracellular matrix is composed of **fibrillary components** (fibers: collagen and elastin) and **ground substance** , which is the matrix in which the cells are dispersed. 

Cartilage has a peculiar feature; it has **no blood vessels** (avascular) and **no nerves** . There is only one exception: the perichondrium (surrounding connective layer, critical for maintenance and formation of cartilage, provides blood) is vascularized. Recall that in the epithelium, another avascular tissue, we also always require a surrounding or underlying connective layer that provides nutrients. 

Breaking a bone is painful because bones have a lot of nerves and are highly vascularized. Breaking or consuming cartilage is not painful, it even happens just through the aging process. The pain is felt after when there is no more cartilage and the bones are touching each other and is being consumed through erosion, but the pain comes from the bones, not from the cartilage. 

### **FUNCTIONS** 

Cartilage has mainly skeletal functions 

- The entire skeletal model of the embryo and fetus is made from cartilage. It will be afterward completely **replaced** by bones. This is a crucial point: there is no transformation between cartilage and bones. They are two different tissues, and they have different cell types and ground matrices. 

- In postnatal development, cartilage allows the growth of long bones ( **metaphyseal cartilage** ). It disappears completely towards the end of the teenage years. Breaking a bone has a better outcome for younger people than older: this is because the cartilage remains in the long bones of a young person, and it allows for the extension of these bones during the development of the skeleton. In adults instead, you stop having so much cartilage and the repair of fractures is more compromised and severe. 



- In adults, cartilage is present on the smooth surface of articular cartilage, such as the knee, where it allows the sliding of the bone surfaces. Articular cartilage has also the peculiarity of not possessing a perichondrium. 

- In adults, cartilage also provides the framework of the outer ear, nose, and some hollow organs of the respiratory systems (trachea, larynx, bronchi) 

The skull of an embryo is the last thing to calcify since we still have brain development.

### **ECM AND CELLS** 

### **Bone Tissue Extracellular Matrix (ECM)** 

The ECM of bone tissue is a composite of both organic and inorganic components, providing strength, flexibility, and rigidity: 

- **Inorganic Matrix (65%)** : Primarily composed of mineral crystals known as hydroxyapatite (containing calcium, phosphate and hydroxide ions), along with approximately **10% water** . This matrix is responsible for the rigidity and hardness of bone, allowing it to protect internal organs and withstand compression. 

- **Organic Matrix (35%)** : The organic portion mainly consists of collagen (23%) and non-collagen proteins (2%). Collagen fibers are essential for providing tensile strength and resistance to forces, ensuring flexibility and resilience. 



The balance between these components makes the bone tissue both strong and flexible. Calcium phosphate crystals, which make up around two-thirds of bone weight, contribute to rigidity, while collagen fibers provide tensile strength. 

### **Impact of ECM Components on Bone Properties** 

The inorganic component of the ECM (minerals) is responsible for the strength and rigidity of bone. Without this component, the tissue could retain tensile strength but would be unable to resist pressure and mechanical stress, leading to structural deformation under load. 

Conversely, the organic component (collagen) imparts resistance to tensile forces. If the organic matrix were compromised, the bone would retain its size and shape but become brittle and fragile, much like porcelain, making it susceptible to fracture. 

In summary, bone tissue's structure and function are intricately related, with the ECM's balanced composition providing the necessary characteristics for bone strength, resilience, and adaptability. 

### **Cellular Organization and Bone Remodeling** 

Bone cells are organized within circular structures called **lamellae** , which are arranged at specific intervals. Within these lamellae, cells have a flattened and elongated structure. This arrangement can be observed in a transverse section of bone, where cells appear relatively large, especially when compared to epithelial cells or fibroblasts. Notably, some cells exhibit plasma membrane extensions, a sign of their dynamic nature. 

The population of cells in bone tissue is heterogeneous, consisting of three main cell types: 

- **Osteoblasts** : Cells responsible for the formation of new bone matrix. 

- **Osteocytes** : Mature bone cells embedded within the matrix, maintaining bone tissue. 

- **Osteoclasts** : Cells involved in the resorption and breakdown of bone tissue. 

The ECM of bone is mineralized, providing the tissue with the resistance, rigidity, and hardness necessary for structural integrity. Despite its hardness, bone remains a dynamic tissue, continuously undergoing remodeling and renewal throughout life. This process is sensitive to environmental factors and individual life conditions, making bone tissue highly adaptable, similar to the immune or nervous systems.

### **Bone Resorption Process (Three Steps):** 

1. **Adhesion:** Osteoclasts adhere to the bone matrix through podosomes. 

2. **Matrix Breakdown:** They secrete hydrogen ions (H+) to create an acidic environment that breaks down the inorganic matrix. 

3. **Enzymatic Degradation:** Lysosomal enzymes degrade the organic matrix. 

Through this process, osteoclasts effectively break down bone tissue to maintain bone remodeling and calcium homeostasis.

Bone is a dynamic tissue that undergoes continuous building, breakdown, and rebuilding through a process called bone remodeling. This process involves two main types of cells: osteoclasts and osteoblasts. 

Osteoclasts are large, multinucleated cells responsible for breaking down and resorbing bone tissue. They originate from monocytes found in the bone marrow. Their primary function is to release minerals and other biologically active molecules stored within the bone matrix. These minerals include calcium phosphate and various growth factors. The release of calcium from the bone plays a critical role in maintaining calcium homeostasis in the body. 

Osteoblasts, on the other hand, are the cells responsible for building new bone tissue. They are believed to originate from cells associated with blood vessels. Once activated, osteoblasts produce osteoid, the organic component of bone tissue, which mainly consists of collagen. Minerals then begin to crystallize around this collagen scaffold to form hydroxyapatite, the primary inorganic component of bone composed of calcium phosphate. 

Bone Mineral Density (BMD) is often used to estimate bone strength and assess the risk of fractures. As osteoblasts form new bone tissue, many of them become embedded within the matrix and differentiate into osteocytes. 

The unique structure, composition, and cellular processes within bone allow it to serve dual purposes: acting as a reservoir for calcium and other essential minerals while providing structural support for vital organs and facilitating locomotion.

### **4. Growth and Regeneration** 

The growth patterns and regenerative abilities of cartilage and bone are also distinct. 

- **Bone growth** occurs through both longitudinal and appositional processes. Longitudinal growth involves the replacement of cartilage by bone during skeletal development, and it is regulated by sex hormones that ultimately cease after puberty. Appositional growth involves the addition of new bone tissue on the outer surface, contributing to the widening of bones. Bone has a remarkable capacity to regenerate and repair itself, although this process slows with age. Bone remodeling, facilitated by osteoclasts and osteoblasts, ensures that bone tissue remains functional and responsive to the mechanical stresses placed upon it. 

- **Cartilage growth** , in contrast, occurs through two processes: appositional growth (from the surface of existing cartilage) and interstitial growth (within the cartilage itself, where chondrocytes divide and produce new matrix). Cartilage has a much slower regenerative capacity than bone and is limited in its ability to repair itself after injury. This makes cartilage more susceptible to damage and degeneration, especially in joints, where conditions such as osteoarthritis can arise. 

### **5. Membranes and Surrounding Structures** 

Both cartilage and bone are surrounded by membranes that serve as protective layers and contain cells that aid in the growth and repair of the tissue. 

- **Cartilage** is enveloped by the **perichondrium** , a fibrous membrane that contains blood vessels and **chondrogenic cells** , which can differentiate into chondroblasts to aid in the growth and repair of cartilage. The perichondrium is the only vascularized part of the cartilage, and its role in cartilage regeneration is crucial. 

- **Bone** is surrounded by two membranes: the **periosteum** and the **endosteum** . The periosteum is a fibrous membrane that covers the outer surface of bones, except at joints, and contains osteoblasts and osteoclasts involved in bone growth and remodeling. The **endosteum** is a thin membrane lining the inner surfaces of bones, including the medullary cavity, and is important for bone nutrition and regeneration. The periosteum also contains **Sharpey’s fibers** , which anchor the periosteum to the bone matrix. 

### **6. Conclusion** 

In summary, while both cartilage and bone are vital components of the musculoskeletal system, their structural, functional, and regenerative properties diverge significantly. Bone provides rigid structural support and protection, is highly vascularized, and has the ability to regenerate, while cartilage offers flexible support and protection, particularly in joints and as a precursor to bone during development. Their distinct compositions, cell types, growth patterns, and regenerative capacities underscore the specialized roles each tissue plays in the body. 

### **3D Printing of Bone Tissue** 

It is theoretically possible to **3D print bone tissue** . The inorganic components of the ECM, such as salts and crystals, can be synthesized and artificially combined with collagen fibers. However, the challenge lies in combining all the different cell types and ensuring their functional integration, which is essential for creating a fully operational tissue or organ.

### **L16/L17: BLOOD** 

Blood is a specialized form of connective tissue in a fluid state, essential for maintaining homeostasis and ensuring the proper physiological functioning of the body. It circulates through the cardiovascular system within a closed network of vessels, transporting various substances and playing a critical role in immunity, coagulation, and homeostatic regulation. Blood is composed of specialized cellular elements, namely erythrocytes (red blood cells), leukocytes (white blood cells), and thrombocytes (platelets), suspended in an extracellular fluid known as plasma.

### **FUNCTIONS OF BLOOD** 

Blood serves several functions essential to the body's survival and optimal functioning: 

1. **Gas Exchange and Transport:** Blood delivers molecular oxygen (O₂) to almost all cells in the body and transports carbon dioxide (CO₂) away from tissues for exhalation by the lungs. 

2. **Nutrient Distribution and Waste Removal:** Blood absorbs and distributes nutrients, electrolytes, and fluids from the intestines to various tissues while simultaneously carrying metabolic waste products to excretory organs such as the kidneys and liver. 

3. **Homeostasis Maintenance:** Blood plays a key role in maintaining a stable internal environment, regulating body temperature, pH, osmotic balance, and blood pressure. 

4. **Immune Defense:** The immune function of blood is facilitated by plasma cells and leukocytes, which recognize and neutralize pathogens and foreign substances. 

5. **Coagulation:** Platelets, which originate as fragments of megakaryocytes, contribute to the coagulation cascade, preventing excessive blood loss in response to vascular injury. 

6. **Hormonal Transport:** Blood carries hormones, allowing distant organs to influence the function and composition of specific tissues, playing a crucial role in systemic homeostasis. A prime example is calcium homeostasis regulated by parathyroid hormone (PTH) and calcitonin, which influence calcium metabolism across various tissues, including bone.

### **Volume and Composition** 

Blood accounts for approximately **7-8% of total body weight** , with an average volume of **4-5 liters in females** and **5-6 liters in males** . The blood pH is tightly regulated around **7.4** , and deviations can lead to pathological states such as **acidosis** (pH < 7) or **alkalosis** (pH > 7.8).

### **Erythrocytes (Red Blood Cells)** 

Erythrocytes are the most abundant cellular component of blood and play a crucial role in oxygen and carbon dioxide transport. The normal erythrocyte count in males is approximately 5 million cells per cubic millimeter (mm³) of blood, while in females, it is about 4.5 million/mm³. These cells have an average lifespan of 120 days, after which they are removed and replaced through erythropoiesis. This continuous turnover ensures the maintenance of efficient oxygen delivery throughout the body. Their size is very small, but also very consistent: it always spans from 7 to 8 micrometers, as to why they are also used as histological rulers. Additionally, they possess surface glycoproteins that determine blood type (A, B, AB, or O), crucial role for transfusion compatibility. 



### **Morphology and Function** 



Erythrocytes have a distinctive biconcave disc shape, which maximizes their surface area for gas exchange and facilitates their ability to deform while navigating through narrow capillaries. Their primary function is to transport oxygen (O₂) from the lungs to tissues and to carry carbon dioxide (CO₂) from tissues back to the lungs. This transport capability is mediated by hemoglobin, a specialized oxygen-binding protein found in high concentrations within the cytoplasm of erythrocytes. 

### **Erythrocyte Deformation and Mechanical Properties** 

The ability of erythrocytes to deform is essential for their function, allowing them to pass through microcapillaries with diameters smaller than the cell itself. This deformability is due to several structural features: 



1. **Absence of nucleus and organelles** : erythrocytes lack nucleus and intracellular organelles, which provides more space for hemoglobin and enhances their flexibility. 2. **Specialized cytoskeleton** : their membrane is reinforced by a complex cytoskeletal network composed of spectrin, actin, and associated proteins, which provide mechanical stability and flexibility to withstand shear force experienced during circulation. 

3. **Pliable membrane** : the lipid bilayer of erythrocytes has 

a large surface to volume ratio, which makes erythrocytes highly elastic. 

The degree of deformation depends on factors such as shear stress, membrane tension, and internal viscosity of the cytoplasm. The image below shows erythrocytes squeezing through capillaries. 

The ability of the erythrocyte to flatten, fold, and elongate reduces mechanical stress and enhances flow efficiency. In conditions where deformability is compromised (e.g. in sickle cell anemia or hereditary spherocytosis), erythrocytes struggle to pass through capillaries, leading to reduced oxygen delivery and potential vascular occlusions.

### **L18: LYMPHATIC TISSUE AND ORGANS** 

The lymphoid tissue is a liquid connective tissue.  Its main purpose is to detect and inactivate foreign substances, such as: 

- Invading microorganisms 

- Cells of transplanted organs 

- Foreign materials eg pollen 

- Cancer cells 

Lymph also brings nutrients from blood vessels to cells and picks up waste products. 

### **THE IMMUNE SYSTEM** 

The overall function of the immune system is to prevent or limit infection by microorganisms and it does that thanks to antigens. 

An **antigen is** a non-self molecule (something that the body recognizes as foreign) or self-modified molecule able to engage or activate specific receptors on surface of B and T lymphocyte. They derive from a very large variety of sources including pathogens, host cells, allergens. They are sentinels for recognizing non-self structures. 

The immune system can distinguish between normal-healthy cells and unhealthy cells by recognizing a variety of "danger" signals called danger-associated molecular patterns ( **DAMPs** ). Infectious microbes such as viruses and bacteria release another set of signals recognized by the immune system called pathogen-associated molecular patterns ( **PAMPs** ). DAMPs and PAMPs stimulate granulocytes, monocytes, macrophages and dendritic cells. 

When the immune system first recognizes "danger" or "pathogen" derived signals, it responds to address the problem. The response can be **innate** or **adaptive** . 

### **The immune system is complex and tightly regulated** 

If an immune response cannot be activated when there is sufficient need, problems arise, like infections. On the other hand, when an immune response is activated without a real threat or it is not turned off once the danger passes, different problems arise: chronic inflammation, allergic reactions, autoimmune diseases. 

Cell types of this tissue play a unique role, with different ways of recognizing problems, communicating with other cells, and performing their functions. 

### **FEATURES OF AN IMMUNE RESPONSE** 

The immune system is generally divided into innate and adaptive response. 

The cells of **innate immunity** are usually **more general,** they are able to recognize **immediately** pathogen or danger to trigger reactions in hours or minutes. 

The **adaptive immunity** takes **longer** (days) and relies on coordination and expansion of **specific** adaptive immune cells. It is much more specific and stays in our body as a **memory** , for these cells to remember the type of interactions they had with a specific pathogen.

### **Oligodendrocytes** 



**Oligodendrocytes** are smaller than astrocytes and neurons, with a round, compact morphology (without visible processes) and a notably prominent **Golgi apparatus** . 

These cells are specialized for **myelination of axons** within the CNS, forming the **myelin sheath** composed of concentric layers of oligodendrocytic plasma membrane. Myelination occurs without the formation of Schmidt-Lanterman incisures, making CNS myelin structurally distinct from PNS myelin. Furthermore, one oligodendrocyte can **myelinate multiple axons** , while **Schwann cells** in the PNS myelinates only a single axon segment. 



Oligodendrocytes are crucial for **saltatory conduction** , providing electrical insulation and significantly increasing the velocity of nerve impulse transmission in the **white matter** . The structural and functional integrity of oligodendrocytes is essential for white matter health, with demyelinating diseases like **multiple sclerosis** directly targeting these cells. 

### **Ependymal Cells** 



**Ependymal cells** form a simple **epithelial-like lining** of the **ventricular system** of the brain and the **central canal** of the spinal cord. These cells originate from neuroepithelium and retain epithelial characteristics. 

Structurally, they are **cuboidal to columnar** and frequently **ciliated** , aiding in the **circulation of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)** within the ventricular spaces 

Ependymal cells play a significant role in the **production and regulation of CSF** , particularly those associated with choroid **plexus** , a vascularized structure located within the ventricles. 



Recent studies have identified the presence of a **vascular barrier within the choroid plexus** , challenging the prior assumption of its permissivity, and revealing its significant role in controlling molecular traffic, especially during **inflammatory conditions** . Moreover, ependymal cells have been recognized as a source of **neural stem cells** , retaining proliferative capacity and contributing to neurogenesis under specific conditions. 

### **Microglia** 

**Microglia** are the resident **immune cells** of the **CNS** , derived from **mesodermal progenitors** of the **monocyte-macrophage lineage** . Entering the CNS during early embryonic development, they are distributed throughout the parenchyma. 



Microglial cells in normal tissue constantly screen their environment. Equipped with several membrane receptors, they can immediately sense signs of disturbed structural and functional integrity. Upon detection of “disturbances” or “danger”, for example tiny vascular or tissue damage, microglia can rapidly respond with a directed reorganization of processes and a change in the activity profile. For instance, microglia can produce neurotrophic factors to support injured neurons, or anti-inflammatory molecules to switch off inflammation.

At the nodes, axon remains covered only by **Schwann cell cytoplasmic projections** of the associated glial cells. 



Historically, arrangement of alternating myelinated internodes and exposed nodes of Ranvier was considered the sole structural configuration along axons. 

However, recent studies, in particular in **mammalian cerebral cortex** , including humans, have identified significant **regional variations** in myelination patterns. These discoveries indicate that axons may exhibit alternating myelinated and unmyelinated segments along their length, even within the CNS. The presence of these atypical patterns suggests that myelination may serve functions beyond increasing conduction velocity. 



One proposed functional implication of these myelination patterns involves their participation in **synaptic plasticity** and **adaptive functional remodeling** . Neural plasticity refers to the capacity of the nervous system to undergo structural and functional changes in response to intrinsic and extrinsic stimuli, including **learning, memory formation,** and **injury recovery** . 

### In this context, **variations in axonal myelination** could play a pivotal **role by regulating the formation, elimination, and remodeling of synaptic connections** . 



Unmyelinated segments of axons may offer additional space for the establishment of new synapses, thereby facilitating enhanced synaptic integration. Furthermore, by selectively modifying the distribution and extent of myelination, the nervous system might dynamically adjust the conduction properties of individual axons, optimizing signal timing and coordination across neural circuits. 

This potential regulatory mechanism implies that myelin is **not** solely a **passive conductor** but may **act as an active modulator** of neuronal connectivity and network function. Although the precise molecular mechanisms and functional outcomes of these myelination variations remain areas of ongoing investigation, accumulating evidence highlights the importance of myelin plasticity as a contributor to the overall adaptability and resilience of the nervous system. 



### **Schmidt-Lanterman Incisures** 

During the process of myelination, residual cytoplasm occasionally remains trapped within the myelin sheath, forming **Schmidt-Lanterman incisures** (or clefts). These are: 

- Discontinuities in the myelin layers, containing cytoplasm. 

- Visible as small, oblique clefts, important for the **maintenance** and **metabolic support** of the myelin sheath.

• **Muscular Dystrophy** : a group of genetic disorders that affect structural proteins of muscles. The most common form, **Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy** , is due to a mutation in the dystrophin gene. Dystrophin links the cytoskeleton of the muscle fiber to the extracellular matrix via the sarcolemma. Its absence leads to **sarcolemmal instability** , progressive fiber damage, and muscle degeneration. Symptoms typically begin in childhood and worsen over time. 

### **Skeletal Muscle Fiber Types and Functional Specialization** 

Skeletal muscle fibers are **heterogeneous** and vary on the type of contractions they produce. They can be divided by their strength, speed, endurance and metabolic profile. They are classified into: 

**1. Type I Fibers (Red, Slow-Twitch)** 

These fibers have high myoglobin content, giving them a red appearance. They rely heavily on **aerobic metabolism** (oxidative phosphorylation), supported by an abundance of mitochondria and dense capillary networks. Type I fibers contract slowly but resist fatigue, making them ideal for **postural muscles** and **endurance** activities like long-distance running. In histological preparations, mitochondria can be visualized running in parallel with the myofibrils. They appear as elongated, dense structures situated near the I bands and Z lines, supporting the regions of high energy demand during contraction. 





### **2. Type II (IIb) Fibers (White, Fast-Twitch)** 

Type II fibers are larger in diameter, contain more myofibrils, but less myoglobin and fewer mitochondria. They generate rapid, powerful contractions but rely on **anaerobic glycolysis** , which uses mainly glycogen as fuel and produces ATP quickly but inefficiently. As a result, they get fatigued easily. These fibers are suited for short **bursts of strength** or **speed** , such as weightlifting or sprinting. 

### **3. Intermediate Fibers (Type IIa)** 

These fibers exhibit features of both type I and II. They are intermediate in size, contract quickly, and have a moderate amount of myoglobin and capillaries, allowing for both **aerobic** and **anaerobic metabolism** . They are involved in activities like **walking** and **jogging** and are more resistant to fatigue than type IIb fibers. They are marked with the letter I in the staining. 

Although every individual’s muscles contain a mixture of these fiber types, the proportions are genetically determined, and athletic training may influence their performance characteristics. For example, endurance training can enhance the oxidative capacity of intermediate fibers. 

### **Muscle plasticity: Hypertrophy and Atrophy** 

Skeletal muscle fibers are **post-mitotic** , meaning they cannot divide; however, they can undergo changes in size and functional capacity in response to physiological demands.

While the existence of functional **cardiac satellite cells** remains debated, it is wellestablished that skeletal muscle satellite cells are central to tissue homeostasis. Stem cell research in this area is ongoing and has implications for regenerative medicine, particularly in muscular dystrophies and sports medicine. 

### **Histological Visualization** 



In **transverse sections** of skeletal muscle, individual striated fibers appear as polygonal structures, with peripheral nuclei visible near their sarcolemma. The **endomysium** is thin and sometimes not visible with light microscopy, while the **perimysium** is bigger and seen as separating bundles of muscle fibers into fascicles. Vessels and nerve fibers can be observed running within the perimysium, providing the necessary infrastructure for cellular function. 



Additionally, sections showing muscle cut in both longitudinal and transverse orientations —typically at 90° to each other— aid in distinguishing muscle fibers from connective tissue, which can sometimes be confused, especially in longitudinal views. Importantly, in true connective tissue areas, **extracellular matrix** is more prominent, **fibroblast nuclei** are elongated and flattened, and cellular prolongations are visible features that distinguish it from muscle fibers. 

### **CARDIAC MUSCLE TISSUE** 

Cardiac muscle tissue is a specialized form of striated muscle that is found exclusively in the **heart** . It functions as the main **muscular pump** responsible for circulating blood throughout the body via the cardiovascular system. The contraction of cardiac muscle ensures that deoxygenated blood is pumped into the lungs, where gas exchange occurs, and oxygenated blood is then propelled into the systemic circulation to nourish tissues and organs. 



Unlike skeletal muscles, cardiac muscle is **involuntarily** controlled. It operates independently and is regulated by both the autonomic nervous system and endocrine signals. For example, **cortisol** , a glucocorticoid, or **epinephrine** from the adrenal medulla, released during stress, can increase cardiac output by acting on cardiac muscle receptors to enhance contractility. Despite this regulation, the **initiation of contraction** in cardiac tissue is **intrinsic** , meaning it does not require direct input from the nervous system to begin contracting. 

### **Heart Wall and layered organization** 

The heart wall is composed **of three concentric layers** : 

**1. Endocardium** : the innermost layer, formed by simple squamous endothelium resting on loose connective tissue. It lines the **chambers** of the **heart** and extends over internal structures such as the valves, chordae tendineae, and papillary muscles.

### **Contraction Characteristics and Functional Implications** 

Smooth muscle contraction exhibits several unique physiological properties: 

- **Slow onset and prolonged contraction** : Smooth muscle contracts more slowly than skeletal or cardiac muscle but is capable of maintaining tone for extended periods with **minimal energy expenditure** due to the **latch state** , in which cross-bridges remain attached with low ATP consumption. 

- **Plasticity** : Smooth muscle cells can undergo large changes in length (as in the bladder or uterus) without losing their ability to generate tension—a property called **stressrelaxation** or **viscoelasticity** . 

- **Coordination** : In organs like the gastrointestinal tract, smooth muscle cells are electrically coupled by gap junctions, allowing coordinated contraction in the form of peristalsis. In the bronchi, similar contraction leads to bronchoconstriction, a key feature in conditions like asthma. 

### **Histological Staining and Microscopy** 

Smooth muscle tissue can be visualized in histological sections using various stains, most commonly **hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)** . In these preparations: 

- The **cytoplasm** of smooth muscle cells typically appears **eosinophilic (pink)** due to its protein content. 

- **Nuclei** stain **basophilic (blue-purple)** due to DNA and RNA binding with hematoxylin. 

Connective tissue elements such as the **endomysium** (surrounding individual smooth muscle fibers) and **perimysium** (surrounding fiber bundles) may also be visible, especially with specific connective tissue stains like **Masson's trichrome** .

|**Feature**|**Smooth Muscle**|**Skeletal Muscle**|
|---|---|---|
|**Control**|Involuntary|Voluntary|
|**Striations**|Absent|Present|
|**Nucleus**|Single, centrally located|Multiple, peripherally located|
|**Cell Type**|Individual, fusiform|Long, cylindrical, multinucleated|
|**Sarcomeres**|Absent|Present|
|**Gap Junctions**|Present (in unitary smooth muscle)|Absent|
|**Contraction Speed**|Slow, tonic or phasic|Rapid, forceful|
|**Regeneration**|Moderate (via mitotic activity)|Limited (via satellite cells)|
